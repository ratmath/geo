Theorems, checked on exact figures

Each function here takes a figure, builds what a theorem talks about, and tests the conclusion with exact arithmetic. holds: true means the statement is true of this figure, with no tolerance involved.

const t = triangle([0, 0], [4, 0], [1, 3]);
theorems.eulerLine(t);        // { theorem, statement, holds: true, O, G, H, … }
theorems.feuerbach(t);        // tangent to the incircle and the three excircles
theorems.ceva(t, D, E, F);    // concurrent exactly when the product of ratios is 1
theorems.ptolemy(A, B, C, D); // equality exactly when the four points are concyclic

What a check is, and is not

A check on one triangle is not a proof for all triangles. It is still worth more than a drawing or a floating-point test, for two reasons. First, the conclusion is an exact equality, so a false statement fails rather than passing within a tolerance. Second, a statement that is an identity between polynomials or algebraic functions of the coordinates and holds at a "generic" exact point is very unlikely to be false. General proofs by elimination, where hypotheses and conclusion become polynomials and the proof is a certificate, are a later phase of this package.

Several of the functions test both directions. ceva reports the product of the ratios and whether the lines are concurrent, and holds says these agree, so it is as informative on a figure where the lines miss as on one where they meet. ptolemy, simson and thales work the same way.

theorems.…statement checked
pythagoras(t)a² + b² = c² exactly when the triangle is right-angled
heron(t)area² = s(s − a)(s − b)(s − c)

ninePointCircle(t)nine points on one circle of half the circumradius

Why Feuerbach's theorem is the hard one

Two circles touch when the distance between their centres equals the sum or the difference of their radii. The nine-point circle of a rational triangle is rational, but the incentre and inradius involve the three side lengths, each a square root, so the distance is a square root of an expression in three square roots, and the test is whether that equals a difference of two more such numbers. In floating point one sees agreement to fifteen digits. Here the two sides are algebraic numbers and the equality is decided from their minimal polynomials.
